1. Introduction
1.1 Purpose of this document
This document describes, in complete detail, what the Manufacturing ERP System must do and how it must
behave. It is the single agreed reference for everyone building, testing, buying or approving the system. If
something is not written here, it is not part of version 1.0 and must go through a change request.
The document answers four questions for every part of the system:  what does the user want to do
(user stories), what data does the system need (field tables), what is not allowed (validation rules),
and what happens step by step (process flows and diagrams).
1.2 The business problem
Small and medium manufacturers in India typically run their factory on a mixture of paper registers, Excel
sheets, WhatsApp messages and a basic accounting package. This causes five recurring problems:
Nobody knows the true stock. Physical stock and book stock differ because issues to production are
not recorded on time.
Orders slip. A sales order is accepted without checking whether raw material or machine capacity is
available.
Costing is guesswork. The real cost of a produced item — material plus scrap plus labour plus
machine hours — is never calculated.
No traceability. When a customer complains about a batch, nobody can trace which raw material lot,
which machine and which operator produced it.
Information does not reach people. The store keeper does not know a dispatch is due today; the
owner does not know a payment is overdue.
This ERP solves all five, and pushes the fifth one out to people's phones through WhatsApp and push
notifications instead of waiting for them to log in.
1.3 Why one product for eleven industries
Plastic moulding, PVC pipe extrusion, corrugated box making, rubber compounding, furniture assembly,
electrical  component  assembly,  auto  part  machining,  steel  fabrication,  aluminium  extrusion,  textile
processing and chemical batch production look very different on the shop floor. But underneath, all eleven
follow the same shape:
Buy raw material  →  Store it  →  Convert it using a recipe and a machine  →  Check
quality  →  Store the finished goods  →  Dispatch and invoice
The differences are in the details: a plastic moulder cares about mould cavity count and cycle time; a pipe
extruder cares about running metres and coil length; a chemical plant cares about batch potency and
expiry; a textile mill cares about shade lots and shrinkage. These are  configuration differences, not
code differences. Section 4 describes the Industry Configuration Layer that turns one codebase into
eleven industry-fit products.
